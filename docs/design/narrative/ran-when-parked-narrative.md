# Ran When Parked: The Narrative Arc

*The Hall of Legends. Locked with the maintainer 2026-08-12. This is deliverable three of
three: the economy brief makes the game work, the cast sheet makes us care, this document
moves the player through it all. It is the game's entire narrative arc, not a side
feature, and is written to the maintainer's standing order: no filler, write meaning.
House rules apply: no em dashes, British English, no decorative Unicode. Companion
documents: `economy-overhaul-brief.md` (D13, D14, section 10), `ran-when-parked-cast.md`.*

---

## 1. The thesis

**In 1995, the canon of Japanese cars does not exist.** Nobody has written it. The West
files Japanese cars under appliances; Japan treats its own masterpieces as used goods;
the bubble's collections are being liquidated abroad and the early heroes are leaving on
boats for good. There is no museum of Japanese motoring, because nobody with money and
conviction has yet stood up and said: these were never products, they were culture.

The Hall of Legends is that act. **It is not a treasury; it is a thesis.** Kurogane is
not collecting the most valuable cars; he is writing the first draft of the canon before
the proof finishes leaving the country. This is the only curatorial logic that can hold
the roster together: real museums do not curate on price, they curate on argument. The
question is never "what is it worth"; it is "does the story of the Japanese automobile
need this chapter?"

This framing resolves the objections that nearly killed the concept. A 2000GT and a
gullwing kei share no metric, but they share a story, one chapter each. And a
basically-new car on a plinth is not a curatorial error; it is a BET, the claim that
makes people laugh at Kurogane in 1995, and the game runs 1995 to 2005: precisely the
decade the world caught up. **The player's own career is the thesis coming true.** By
the end, the hall they laughed at is the hall they queue for. The decade clock does the
vindication for free.

The theme, inherited from the cast sheet and completed here: things worth saving, saved,
before anyone else agrees they are worth it.

## 2. Chapter zero: the bet

The arc begins before day one, and the player is its first acquisition.

Kurogane needed hands. His line to Gonda, the founding sentence of the whole game:
**"I need a good pair of hands. Set them up with a space, and stand for them at the
auction house."** Gonda chose the player and wagered on the choice. The lockup, the
lease, the Local Yard introduction: none of it was kindness. The shop exists because the
Hall required a builder, and Gonda bet an old friend that this one would do.

**Structure of the early game:** Kurogane stays dark (canon: the-quiet-crate is held).
The first chapters arrive DISGUISED, through Gonda as intermediary: a quiet buyer paying
proper money for a perfect, oddly exacting restoration of a worthless old car, and the
Mechanic's monologue allowed to be suspicious about it.

**The audition (D16).** Kurogane did not commission a wrench; he commissioned a
JUDGMENT. Through Gonda, across the disguised years, he has been watching how the player
handles the doubt-follows-the-car decisions (economy brief 9.6): what they disclose, what
they pass on, whether their yes means anything. The sale record already exists; the story
simply reads it back.

**The reveal**, mid-career, around the Premium era: the player walks into a half-empty
hall and finds their own early work on the plinths. They have been building the museum
for years. Three truths land at once: your work was always part of something, the
something is why you exist, and the quiet buyer was grading your honesty the whole time.
From that day the empty plinths are the quest log, made diegetic.

## 3. The nine plinths

The count is derived, twice, and is therefore not arbitrary: the argument has exactly
this many beats, and the culture has exactly this many tribes with faces in the cast.
Cut a chapter and a sentence is missing from the thesis; add one and a beat repeats.

Each chapter: its name (see the reveal rule in section 4), its argument, its build, its
face, and its hard axis. Difficulty is NOT price; a chapter is hard on exactly one of
three axes until the last, which is hard on all three:

- **Money**: acquiring and funding it (the D4 endgame sink landing).
- **Build**: the restoration or modification itself, at flagship scale (enormous after
  the basket inversion, economy brief 9.9).
- **Proof**: establishing that the car is what it claims to be (economy brief section
  10's discovery-through-work mechanics, plus the authored missions here (section 7)).

Notes that carry design weight:

- **Chapter 9 is chronologically EARLY and structurally LAST.** The 2000GT is not the
  newest legend; it is the keystone that is absent. The Hall is built around its empty
  central plinth from the reveal onward. Every other chapter is an argument; this one is
  a hole where the founding evidence should be.
- **The modification chapters (5, 7, and 2's race trim) are load-bearing for the
  thesis.** A museum of stock cars would argue the culture is factories. These chapters
  argue it is what people DID with them, which is the truer claim and the one the whole
  game makes.
- **Chapter 4 is the cast sheet's Gonda arc completing onto a plinth**, and deliberately
  costs the player nothing but the years it took to earn it.
- **Chapters commission arguments, never chassis (D16):** the table's build column names
  what the chapter needs, and the PLAYER sources the actual car through normal play
  (auction find, barn discovery, rebuilt wreck) and nominates it. The museum enshrines
  what the player chose; the hunt is the game already built.
- Car types are named by type, not roster id: mapping to the parody roster is a content
  task at authoring time (roster doc is the source of truth, CLAUDE.md directive 24).

## 4. The pacing laws

1. **One chapter is always live** from year one to the end. Never a burst, never a
   drought. The arc is the career's spine, not its epilogue.
2. **Chapters gate off the guarantor ladder that already exists** (auction-guarantors.md):
   disguised chapters off Local and Regional, the reveal at Premium, the late chapters
   behind the Collector Network. No new gating machinery.
3. **Chapter names are revealed only on completion** (maintainer ruling): the plaque is
   unveiled when the build is done, naming what the player has just proven. Before
   completion a chapter is only a commission and a car.
4. **The three difficulty axes rotate** (money, build, proof) so consecutive chapters
   never feel alike; the table above fixes each chapter's axis.
5. **Shippability**: the structure ratifies independently of the count. The arc can ship
   with the disguised chapters plus the reveal and grow; an empty plinth is not missing
   content, it is a promise, and the fiction says so out loud.

## 5. The agency laws (D16)

The player must never be a courier in their own story. Four rules, cheapest first:

1. **The player chooses the car.** Chapters commission the argument; the player nominates
   the chassis (section 3). The deepest agency available at near-zero cost: the player is
   not building the museum, they are choosing what history remembers.
2. **The player interprets the chapter.** Each chapter admits two or three authored
   readings (the hachiroku as the delivery boy's honest wear, or as the mountain replica)
   and the player's pick sets the build target and the plaque text. Kurogane accepts
   either: the Hall commissions the chapter, the Mechanic writes it.
3. **The audition was of the player's choices** (section 2): the disguised era graded
   judgment, not craft, and the reveal recontextualises the player's own systemic
   behaviour as the story.
4. **The ending is the player's** (section 7): the final plaque is a choice, and both
   answers are earned.

## 6. The fraud mechanics (how a car is discovered to be a lie)

Specified in the economy brief, section 10, and summarised here because the endgame
depends on them. There is ONE diagnosis system. Identity participates in it three ways:

- **Pre-purchase**: identity rungs in ordinary symptom ladders (the filler line, the
  odometer that disagrees with the pedal wear, the brochure the car cannot match), priced
  at the room's odds like any doubt, straddle-checked so knowledge can flip the buy.
- **Post-purchase**: the truth reveals itself THROUGH THE WORK, guaranteed, never quizzed
  (P6). The sander finds the accident; the teardown finds the wrong internals; the paint
  strip finds the second colour. Discovery converts directly into the core loop's live
  decision (economy brief 9.6): fix it honest, disclose it, or pass it on with a witness.
- **Endgame**: authored authentication missions, bespoke set pieces in which the cast are
  the instruments: Gonda's memory of every car that died, Nagata's certified specs, and,
  for one scene per legend, the retired assembly-line judge who runs a glove along a
  doorjamb and says "that's not how we did it." Authentication by the people who were
  there.

## 7. The 2000GT (the endgame, locked)

### 7.1 The record

October 1966, the parody-Yatabe high-speed trial: a 2000GT runs for 72 hours straight,
day and night, setting three world records and thirteen international speed-and-endurance
marks, and keeps running when a typhoon crosses the track mid-trial. The car that would
not stop; the announcement of Japan to the world; the founding evidence of the canon.
This record type is load-bearing, not flavour: an endurance trial attaches to ONE
certified chassis and engine, documented almost entirely in certification paperwork and
factory logs. It is exactly the kind of fame that lives in an archive one trusted broker
can bend. (Names are parodied per the roster rules; the substance keeps real history's
shape.)

### 7.2 The lie, in numbers

Exact digits are authorable at content time; the structure is locked.

- **The true pairing, certified in 1966:** chassis MF10-10045, engine 3M-1102.
- **1970:** a collector wants the record car; it cannot be had. Kurogane takes an
  ordinary 2000GT (chassis 10078, engine 3M-1182), re-stamps its CHASSIS to 10045, and
  leaves the engine untouched, because a re-stamped block is where forgeries die. Then
  the move only the broker holding the provenance file could make: **he bends the archive
  itself by one digit, so the certification now reads engine 3M-1182.** Paper and
  substitute agree perfectly. The car sails; the record vouches for the fraud; his career
  is made.
- **The erasure:** the TRUE car, still in Japan wearing 10045 and 1102, is now officially
  abroad. Every inspection of it concludes the obvious: a clone of the famous exported
  car. **The real record car spends twenty-five years being rejected as a fake of
  itself**, sold cheap, laughed out of serious garages, once nearly reaching Gonda's
  yard. Kurogane did not lose it. He erased it, and cannot search for it openly without
  confessing.
- **The trap this creates:** forgers build to the public record, and the record contains
  his lie. Therefore a candidate matching the record PERFECTLY is a fake, and the true
  car is the only one that contradicts the record in exactly the one small place he bent
  it. His sin is the one signature that cannot be forged, because only its author knows
  it exists.

### 7.3 The reveal, in three beats (one candidate each; the player makes the deduction)

**Beat one: the fake. The player's craft wins.** A candidate arrives perfect on paper
(10045, 3M-1182, matching the record digit for digit). The work finds the shed anyway: a
fresh stamp under old paint, a wrong-era weld, through the same discovery-through-work
verbs the player has used all game. Verdict: fake, caught on metal. Skills rewarded, and
the beat states its lesson in the ledger, hidden from nobody: **this forger built the car
to match the public record, digit for digit. Forgers copy papers.**

**Beat two: the true car. The player holds two facts and chooses.** Found through
Gonda's memory: the 2000GT the whole trade laughs at, the famous fake. The work comes
back honest EVERYWHERE (tired-Friday stamps, period welds, real wear) except one ledger
line: **metal says engine 1102; the record says 1182. One digit.** The game does not
auto-verdict and nobody overrules anyone. The conflict goes to the player as the
decision it is: **which witness do you believe, the metal or the paper?** The whole game
has trained one answer. A player applying the game's own thesis makes the leap
themselves: if the metal is true, the record is wrong, records do not bend on their own,
and exactly one person was holding the file in 1970. **The player walks into the
warehouse with the accusation.** The last case of the detective game, and the culprit is
the client. (P6 holds: a player who does not make the leap is not gated; choosing the
metal carries them to the same scene and Kurogane's reaction does the rest. The
deduction is a reward, never a toll.)

**Beat three: proof, confession, key.** One scene. Kurogane confirms: twenty-six years
old, one digit, the substitute that sailed, the real car he erased into a counterfeit of
itself. **Nagata produces the second witness:** the powertrain department's own trial
test sheet, engine 3M-1102 in the factory's hand, written years before 1970 and beyond
anyone's reach to bend. Two independent sources against one bent archive: the verdict
inverts on EVIDENCE, not on tears, and the Mechanic's reading of the metal is vindicated,
never overruled. The ninth plinth fills.

**The plaque (the ending, and the player's).** The player holds the truth and writes the
final plaque. Tell it honestly, and the canon opens with a fraud confessed, Kurogane's
name taking the wound he has owed for twenty-five years. Keep the legend clean, and the
truth stays between two people and an engine. No wrong answer; both endings earned; the
Hall runs either way. It is the game's thesis as a final question: papers or metal,
which one is the truth FOR? That is the Mechanic's chapter, and its name is revealed by
what the player chooses.

### 7.4 The last rite

**Every car in the Hall runs.** Not preserved: running, started, warm. The shop's
signature on the thesis, the reason a museum needed a mechanic, and the landing of the
title: the game is named for the used-car ad's oldest hopeful lie, and it ends in the
one building in Japan where the phrase is never needed, turning the key on the car whose
entire claim to history is that it ran for three days and would not stop. It ran. It
still runs.

## 8. The delivery law (how the arc stays legible without spoonfeeding)

The maintainer's constraint: a slightly disinterested player who skims every text block
must still be in the loop, without Netflix-style exposition. The structural advantage
first: **the load-bearing knowledge is trained, not told.** The arc hinges on one belief
(metal tells the truth, paper lies) and no player can skip it, because it lives in five
hundred repetitions of the core loop, not in a text box. On that floor, six rules:

1. **Three channels per critical beat.** Every load-bearing fact lands as a system event
   (ledger line), a character line, and an image. A skimmer misses one channel, never
   all three. Nothing plot-critical rides on a single line of text.
2. **The one-line law.** No load-bearing beat may need more than two short lines to
   land ("Record says 1182. Block says 1102."), and critical lines live at moments of
   forced attention: verdict screens, plaque unveilings, build completions. Never in
   skippable conversation middles. The Vimes register is legibility engineering, not
   just tone.
3. **The finale speaks the game's oldest visual sentence.** The whole UI thesis is two
   numbers disagreeing (the room's price against yours, since the tutorial). Beat two
   renders as exactly that trained shape: the paper's number beside the metal's number.
   Parsing it IS the game; it cannot be missed.
4. **The choice is the comprehension checkpoint.** A cutscene can be skimmed; a question
   addressed to the player cannot. "Which do you believe, the metal or the paper?"
   forces even a disengaged player to load the mystery's state, doing the recap's job
   without a recap existing.
5. **Foreshadowing pays twice, and the second payment carries the skimmer.** Planted
   lines are spoken back at the payoff ("I told you the papers were lying about
   something small. I never said I did not know what."), so the attentive get the chill
   of recognition and the skimmer gets the meaning fresh. Both audiences, one line.
6. **Gonda is the recap system, and the plinth is the quest log.** At each beat
   transition Gonda compresses the case into one blunt quotable line; between beats, the
   empty central plinth restates the stakes wordlessly on every Hall visit.

The fine line, located: spoonfeeding is mostly a DISTANCE error. Critical links get
short setup-to-payoff distance (beat one's lesson pays off in beat two, still warm);
only flavour gets long distance (the 1970 line planted at first meeting). Keep the
critical chain short and triple-channelled and nothing ever needs restating.
